The 90-Year Problem: Understanding Navier-Stokes Equations

Reading time: 5 minutes | Updated September 26, 2026

The Navier-Stokes equations are the mathematical rulebook for predicting how fluids move—water flowing through pipes, air swirling around planes, blood flowing through arteries. For nearly a century, scientists have used these equations to model everything from weather patterns to aircraft design.

But here's the problem: mathematicians never proved the equations actually work. They don't know if smooth, well-behaved fluids can suddenly develop singularities—points where motion breaks down completely.

Why Does This Matter?

In 2000, the Clay Mathematics Institute named the Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness problem as one of seven Millennium Prize Problems. Each problem comes with a $1 million reward.

The Two Sub-Problems:
  • Smoothness/Existence: Do smooth solutions always exist? (UNSOLVED)
  • Breakdown/Singularity: Can fluids develop singularities in finite time? (SOLVED by OpenAI in 2026)
Source: Clay Mathematics Institute

A Brief History

Claude-Louis Navier and George Gabriel Stokes developed these equations in the 19th century. In 1934, mathematician Jean Leray proved that solutions exist in a generalized sense. But the question of whether they always remain smooth remained unanswered—for 92 years.

Why Mathematicians Care

Proving (or disproving) singularity formation isn't just academic—it has real consequences for:

  • Climate modeling: Understanding extreme weather patterns
  • Aerodynamics: Designing safer aircraft
  • Medical imaging: Modeling blood flow in cardiovascular disease
  • Engineering: Predicting turbulence and flow breakdown
Sources: Clay Mathematics Institute | Nature Magazine Report

The Breakthrough: How 10,000 AI Agents Solved It in 88 Hours

Reading time: 6 minutes | Updated September 26, 2026

On September 8, 2026, OpenAI announced something unprecedented: a proof that smooth fluids CAN develop singularities in finite time.

The method? Not a single genius mathematician working for decades. Instead: roughly 10,000 autonomous AI agents, running in parallel, solved it in 88 hours.

The Computational Approach

Here's how it worked:

  • 10,000 concurrent agents subdivided into groups with inter-group communication
  • Parallel exploration: Each agent tried different mathematical approaches simultaneously
  • 88 hours: From September 1 to September 5, 2026
  • Formal verification: The proof was checked and formalized in Lean 4 programming language
What This Means:

The AI system proved that under certain conditions—specifically, with an externally applied force—a smooth fluid can develop infinite velocity (a singularity) before time actually "ends." This resolves one of the two sub-problems posed by the Clay Institute.

Source: OpenAI Official Statement | Nature Magazine | CNBC Report

The Math Behind the Breakthrough

This proof builds on foundational work by mathematicians Diego Córdoba (Institute for Mathematical Sciences, Madrid) and Luis Martínez-Zoroa (CUNEF University, Madrid), who developed a novel strategy—the "infinite cascade" method—that fundamentally changed how researchers approach the problem.

Instead of traditional approaches, they showed how stacking ordinary solutions could create pathological behavior when assembled—a purely analytical insight that OpenAI's AI agents then formalized and verified.

Important Note on the Solution:

The OpenAI proof uses an externally applied force on the fluid. This means the singularity doesn't occur naturally—it requires external intervention. Some mathematicians focus on the unforced problem (without external forces), which remains unsolved.

Source: OpenAI Official Statement

Why AI Agents Work Better Than Humans Here

Traditional mathematical proof requires one key ingredient: creative insight. A human mathematician might spend 20+ years exploring dead-end approaches before finding the right framework.

AI agents don't get tired. They can:

  • Explore 10,000 approaches in parallel (vs. one at a time)
  • Follow logical chains without fatigue or emotional attachment to failed ideas
  • Verify results formally using the Lean programming language—a guarantee that can't be faked
  • Combine insights from multiple partial solutions into one coherent proof
Speed Achievement:

88 hours vs. ~90 years of human effort = 8,000x faster

Even accounting for the cost of computation, the time savings alone make this a historic achievement.

What About the $1 Million Prize?

The Priority Dispute: Who Really Gets Credit?

Reading time: 5 minutes | Updated September 26, 2026

The Navier-Stokes solution wasn't as straightforward as OpenAI's announcement suggested. Behind the headline is a story of overlapping research, priority disputes, and the complicated attribution of mathematical discoveries in the AI era.

The Foundational Work: Córdoba & Martínez-Zoroa

The real heroes are mathematicians, not the AI.

Diego Córdoba (Institute for Mathematical Sciences, Madrid) and Luis Martínez-Zoroa (CUNEF University, Madrid) spent years developing a radically new approach to the Navier-Stokes problem. Instead of attacking it head-on, they created the "infinite cascade" method—showing how to stack ordinary solutions to create pathological (singular) behavior.

This wasn't an AI discovery. It was pure analytical mathematics, published and discussed in the academic community before OpenAI's work.

Source: Quanta Magazine | Nature Magazine

The Concurrent Work: Buckmaster & Alpöge

On the morning of September 8, 2026, OpenAI announced its Navier-Stokes solution. But 12 hours earlier, mathematician Tristan Buckmaster (New York University) and Levent Alpöge (Anthropic) announced they had solved several closely related problems using a variety of AI models.

According to Quanta Magazine's investigation, Buckmaster's work came first, and OpenAI may have pursued their particular approach after learning about Buckmaster's results.

Priority Dispute:

Was this a discovery race? Did OpenAI follow Buckmaster's lead? The mathematical community is still working through questions of attribution, priority, and how to credit work done by humans versus AI systems.

Source: Quanta Magazine Investigation | Implicator Analysis

The Key Question: Has It Been Solved?

Technically? Yes, one of two sub-problems.

OpenAI proved finite-time singularity formation exists with an external force. But many mathematicians care more about the unforced problem—whether singularities can occur naturally.

Status as of September 26, 2026:
  • ✅ SOLVED (forced): Singularities CAN form with external force
  • ❓ UNSOLVED (unforced): Can they form without external forces?
  • ❓ UNSOLVED (smoothness/existence): Do smooth solutions always exist?

The Clay Mathematics Institute still lists the problem as "active"—meaning independent verification is ongoing and no prize has been awarded.
